When selecting a color palette for your 3D-printed Sears-style houses, it is helpful to distinguish between the two primary philosophies of the early 20th century: the "honest" **Arts & Crafts/Craftsman** aesthetic and the more traditional **Colonial Revival** style.
### 1. Arts & Crafts / Craftsman Palette (1900–1930)
Since many Sears kit homes were Bungalows or Craftsman-style, this is likely your most "period-authentic" choice. This palette emphasizes colors found in nature to help the home harmonize with its surroundings.
- **Body Colors:** Earth tones are key. Think deep, muted greens (sage, moss, olive), rich browns (chocolate, umber, tan), and dark, stony greys.
- **Trim & Accents:** Rather than stark white, trim was often painted in a creamy off-white or a slightly lighter/darker shade of the body color.
- **Key "Pop" Colors:** Deep reds (brick, rust), copper, or muted golds were often used for doors, window sashes, and decorative accents to mimic the appearance of stained wood or ceramic tiles.
- **Vibe:** Honest, organic, and grounded.
### 2. Colonial Revival Palette (1880–1930s)
If your model represents a more formal Sears house, the Colonial Revival aesthetic is highly appropriate. This period saw a shift back toward lighter, more "polite" colors.
- **Body Colors:** Predominantly white, cream, or light straw/ochre. Soft "stone" colors like pale grey or light blue-grey were also common.
- **Trim:** Generally a crisp white or cream that creates a strong contrast with the body of the house.
- **Sash & Doors:** This is where the drama happens—dark green, black, or deep navy were standard for shutters, doors, and window sashes to provide a sharp, formal outline.
- **Vibe:** Formal, airy, and structured.

### Practical Tips for N-Scale Structures
- **Scale Contrast:** Remember that in N-scale, colors can sometimes appear "flatter" due to lighting. If you go with a muted Arts & Crafts palette, consider slightly increasing the contrast between your body and trim colors so the architectural details don't disappear at a distance.
- **Avoid "Pure" White:** Unless you are doing a very specific 1950s-style house, avoid modern "super-bright" white. A "bone" or "antique white" will look much more authentic to the lead-based paints of the 1920s.
- **The "Sash" Rule:** Regardless of the style, keep the window sashes (the frames immediately around the glass) dark. Historically, this made the windows appear as dark voids, which was a standard architectural technique to give the house depth and definition.
