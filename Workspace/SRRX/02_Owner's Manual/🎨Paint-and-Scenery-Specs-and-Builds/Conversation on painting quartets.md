***Here is the recommendation for your primer and base coat sequence, optimized to preserve detail while setting up the Zenithal method:***
1. **The Primer Choice: Airbrush Gray, Skipping the Rattle Can**
	For a highly detailed O-scale print that you want to gift to your quartet mates, you must use the **airbrush gray primer.**
	Relying on the Rustoleum rattle can is an enormous risk in this scenario. Rattle cans are designed for high throughput and rapid coverage, not precision. The resulting paint film is far too thick for model work. If all four figures are a single piece, you have countless intricate recessed areas (the gaps between legs, under lapels, and between faces and shirts) that a rattle can will instantly flood and fill with paint.
	The airbrush primer uses micro-fine pigment and allows you to apply an ultra-thin, smooth, matte coat that retains 100% of the printed detail. It takes longer, but the quality difference is absolute.
2. **The Base Underlayment: Gray, Skip the Tan**
	For this specific reference image, middle gray is the optimal underlayment.
	While dark tan is excellent for warm skin tones and the brown/tan jackets, it makes painting the gray, black, and crisp white elements significantly harder. You would be fighting the warm "under-temperature" of the tan base coat for the rest of the paint session, requiring heavy layering (which, again, risks obscuring detail) to achieve the neutral or cool tones of the charcoal trousers and white shoes.

Excellent. Now that the structural lighting is permanently baked into the primer layer, we move to the color phase.
The golden rule for painting over a Zenithal prime is transparency. If you apply standard, opaque hobby paint straight out of the bottle, you will instantly obliterate all that beautiful pre-shading work you just did. The color layers act as tinted glass—they change the hue, but let the gray/white values underneath dictate the shadows and highlights.
Here are the two ways to tackle this, depending on what is currently sitting on your paint rack:
	**Option 1:** The "Speedpaint" Route (Highly Recommended)
		Paint lines like Citadel Contrast, Army Painter Speedpaint, or Vallejo Xpress Color are chemically engineered specifically for Zenithal undercoats.
	**How they work:** They are heavily pigmented but highly translucent. When you brush them over the model, they automatically tint the white highlights while flowing into the gray/dark recesses to create deep, rich shadows in a single coat.
	**The Verdict:** If you are trying new techniques out of the box, this is the closest thing to magic for capturing the folds in those suit jackets.
	**Option 2:** The Glazing Route (Using Standard Acrylics)
		If you are using standard opaque miniature acrylics (like Vallejo Model Color, Reaper, or standard craft paints), you have to turn them into glazes yourself.
	**How it works:** You must heavily thin the paint on a palette using an acrylic medium (or water, though medium is better for adhesion) until it has the consistency of skim milk.
	**The Verdict**: It takes more time and often requires two or three ultra-thin coats to build up the color without covering the Zenithal map, but it gives you total control over the exact tint of the charcoal trousers and specific jacket hues.
#### **The "Inside-Out" Sequence**
Because this is a single 4-figure piece, accessing tight spots without bumping wet paint on a neighboring figure is going to be your biggest mechanical hurdle. Paint from the "inside out" and from the skin down:
**Skin Tones** (Faces and Hands): Get the faces done first. If you accidentally get a little flesh tone on a white shirt collar, it is easy to cover up later.

### 1. The Safety Rule for Cleaning & Prep (No Solvents)
Before a drop of primer touches the resin/plastic, wipe the unified model down gently with a microfiber cloth slightly dampened with 91% isopropyl alcohol to remove any residual mold release or finger oils. Let it flash dry completely. Do not submerge or soak it.
### 2. The Foolproof Primer Phase: Airbrush Only
- **Do not touch the Rustoleum rattle can.** On a multi-figure unified block, rattle cans lack the fine atomization control required and will pool in the tight crevices between the legs and laps.
- **The Mix:** Use your **Airbrush Gray Primer** straight (or thinned by no more than 5-10% with airbrush thinner if your specific bottle requires it).
- **The Pressure:** Set your compressor to **15–18 PSI**.
- **The Execution:** Hold the piece about 4 to 6 inches away. Apply two ultra-light, misty coats, allowing 5 minutes of drying time between them. You want a uniform, matte gray shell that preserves every microscopic detail of the faces and suit lapels.
### 3. Practicing the Reverse Zenithal (Step B & C)
Since you are nervous about messing up the lighting gradient, **do not practice on the real model.** Find a scrap piece of 3D-printed plastic or a spare test part with similar vertical geometry, prime it gray, and run through the low-angle shadow pass (Step B) and top-frontal light mist (Step C) first. Once you see how the gradient behaves under your airbrush, you will have total confidence for the real run.
### 4. Controlling the Paint Flow for the Jackets
When you start applying your MicroLux airbrush colors as glazes using a regular paintbrush:
- **The Wet Palette Rule:** Put your paint on a wet palette (or a smooth tile) and wick your brush on a paper towel _before_ touching the model. The brush should feel almost dry to the touch—this forces the paint into a true transparent glaze that respects the Zenithal shadows underneath rather than covering them up like an opaque wall.
