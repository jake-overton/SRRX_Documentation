# Foreword

*This handbook describes a railroad that does not exist.*

*The Silver Ridge Railway has never carried ore down from a mine on the northwest face of any mountain. The Valley Flyer has never crossed a gorge in the autumn light, its observation platform open to the receding canyon. Pine Valley Junction is a yard built on a table in a room, and the crews who work it are, at any given moment, one person with a throttle and however much imagination they are willing to bring to the work.*

What I was trying to build — what this handbook is the record of — is a place where that imagination has something to push against. A railroad with a reason to exist. Industries that depend on it. A community that would notice if it stopped running. The Valley Flyer earns its operating priority not because a rule says so, but because it matters to the people of Silver Ridge in the way that a passenger train on a small railroad matters: it is the public face of something that would otherwise be invisible. The ore trains pay the bills. The Flyer keeps the lights on in a different sense entirely.

The SRRX is set in the diesel era of the Rocky Mountain shortline — the 1970s through the 2000s, a period when railroads like this one were surviving by being stubborn and useful rather than efficient or modern. That felt like the right era for a railroad built on a mountain, serving customers that a larger carrier would have abandoned, holding on because the alternative was worse for everyone. The equipment is secondhand. The grades are steep. The crews know every curve by feel. None of that is incidental to the railroad's character — it is the character.

There are several places whose names and existence have a personal connection to me, making this railroad more than just a hobby creation. First, there is a freight car in the fleet bearing the Burlington Route logo. Growing up, there was a book on my father's bookshelf written by his uncle, Richard Carlton Overton, called *The Burlington Route* and carried that logo. Uncle Dick was a historian and a railroad enthusiast. This was one of a few books he wrote on railroads. That logo was superseded several times over as the Burlington Route ultimately became BNSF, the Class I that still operates today.

In a shout out to my father, also named Dick, is the bar/diner with the name Dew Drop Inn. This was the name of the bar that my father would stop by at on his way back from the NY Giants games with whoever he talked into going with him to watch Y.A. Tittle and Fran Tarkenton lead the Giants to yet another loss, coincidentally about the same time frame that I am modeling the SRRX. I believe the bar was by the river on the "other side of the tracks" in Weehawken, NJ, a very blue collar area of the Garden State.

The inspiration for Silver Ridge always was from my love for the Colorado mountains, and in particular from Steamboat Springs, where my oldest son lives with his wife and my (currently) only grandson. He is the CFO of the local community bank, the Yampa Valley Bank. Steamboat Springs is a big little town that exists mainly because the railroad came through in 1909 allowing Steamboat to export coal, shipping and receiving livestock, mineral, and other agricultural supplies. Passenger service stopped in 1968. Today freight no longer stops in Steamboat, as the Union Pacific rumbles through using the historic freight line to cross the state.

My younger son works at a large government contractor that maintains discreet offices across the world. In his honor, I have modeled a deliberately unremarkable building that no one could possibly miss, with blacked out windows, a satellite dish, an obvious government vehicle, and a barbed wire fence on a hill just outside of town.

Lastly, the gorge flagstop depot is modeled after a converted passenger car, from the late 19th century, called an Overton Coach. The name came from William Dayton Overton (no relation that I know of) who built lightweight wooden passenger cars in the 1870s-80s. They were common on short lines and narrow gauge roads in exactly the kind of mountain territory the SRRX operates in. Somehow, it just felt right to use a car named after an Overton on this railroad, even if the connection is coincidental.

The railroad does not need these personal connections to run. But they are part of why it was worth building.

If you are reading this as a guest operator, the handbook will tell you what you need to know about how the railroad runs. The rules are in Section 7. The switching guides are in Section 8. The timetable is in the appendix. What the handbook cannot tell you is that the Mine Run is the most satisfying movement on the layout, and that the Valley Flyer crossing the gorge bridge — even at switching speed, even on a table in a room — is worth pausing for. You will figure that out on your own.

If you are reading this after I am no longer around to explain it, then I hope the railroad speaks for itself. The track plan is v10. Everything in this handbook was put there on purpose, even the things that look like workarounds. Especially those.

The Silver Ridge Railway exists because someone stood beside imaginary tracks and waited for a distant horn that would never roll through a real mountain. It turns out that is enough of a reason. Run the trains accordingly.

— Jake Overton
