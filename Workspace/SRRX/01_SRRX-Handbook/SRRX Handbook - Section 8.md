# SECTION 8 – Train Movements and Switching Guides

The Silver Ridge Railway exists because of geography. The mountain between Pine Valley Junction and Silver Ridge is not a barrier that roads have solved — not reliably, not year-round, and not for the tonnage that keeps the communities on both sides of it alive. The mine needs its empties and needs its loads taken away. The smelter needs its chemical inputs. The Fuel Depot needs tank cars. The town of Silver Ridge needs lumber, dry goods, mail, and the kind of bulk freight that a mountain road cannot carry in January. The national park needs its service materials. None of this moves efficiently without the railroad, and the railroad exists to move all of it.

Section 8 is the operational playbook for how that work gets done. Where Section 7 establishes the rules, Section 8 describes the practice — how trains are assembled, how cars are worked, and how the railroad fulfills its obligations to the customers and communities it serves. The procedures here are written for the crew that already knows the Rule Book. They assume that authority has been obtained, that the mainline is clear, and that the basic disciplines of Section 7 are second nature. What follows is the work itself.

## 8.1. The Mine Run
The Mine Run is the Silver Ridge Railway's most demanding regular movement, and the one that most clearly defines what kind of railroad the SRRX is. It requires two engines, covers three switchback legs on grades that reach 3.3 percent, and offers no runaround at the top. Every decision about car counts and cut points has to be made before the road engine leaves Pine Valley Junction, because there is no room for improvisation once the consist is on the mountain.

The run is a two-engine operation. The road engine is the primary motive power, it makes the climb, works the mine, and brings the loaded cars back down to the smelter. The helper is needed for the ascent, providing additional push on the lower grade before cutting off. It is also used to assist in pushing the loaded cars to the smelter.

The mine spur leaves the north leg in the northeast corner of the layout and climbs in three switchback legs through two turnouts to the mine terminus at the base of Silver Ridge Mountain. The grades — 2.7, 3.3, and 2.6 percent across the three legs respectively — are manageable for a properly sized consist but unforgiving of sloppy car handling or slack running. The standard consist is two cars. Three is the maximum, and that decision belongs to the road engine crew before departure, not on the mountain.

At the mine terminus there are two tracks. Spur A is the active track — loaded ore cars staged and ready for pickup. Spur B is the holding track, where empty cars wait until they can be repositioned for the next loading cycle. The distinction matters because there is no runaround at the top. The road engine arrives with empties, spots them to Spur B, then repositions to couple onto the loads on Spur A. The sequence has to work in that order every time.

Departing PVJ, the road engine leads the empty consist onto the mine spur with the helper coupled to the rear, pushing from behind. Together they work the lower leg at restricted speed — no slack running on grade — up to the first switchback turnout. This is where the helper cuts off. The standard cut point is Turnout 1, though the crew may elect Turnout 2 if the grade or consist weight warrants. The call belongs to the road engine crew, confirmed by radio before uncoupling. Once the helper cuts off, it descends light to the turnout for the smelter. If there are empty cars at the smelter, the helper ascends to pick them up and take them to PVJ yard and returns to the smelter turnout. The mine spur above Turnout 1 belongs to the road engine from that point forward.

The road engine works the remaining two legs alone, reversing direction at each switchback. Restricted speed through both upper legs, no exceptions. At the mine terminus the engine spots the empties to Spur B, clears the turnout, and couples onto the loaded cars on Spur A. Brakes are tested before the descent begins. The road engine brings the loaded consist back down through the switchbacks to the turnout for the Silver Smelter. The helper and road engine then push the loaded cars to the smelter.

The mine spurs do not reset themselves. With loaded cars removed and empties sitting on Spur B, the tracks need to be repositioned before the next revenue pull can happen. 

Two operational constraints govern every Mine Run without exception. There is no runaround at the mine terminus under any circumstances, which means the spotting sequence — empties to Spur B before coupling to loads on Spur A — is not optional. And the mine spur above the cut point is single-engine territory once the helper has descended.

## 8.2. The Valley Flyer
The Valley Flyer is a different kind of railroad from everything else in Section 8. The ore trains and the local freights exist to move tonnage. The Flyer exists to move people, and it does so on a schedule that the rest of the railroad is obligated to respect. Its operating pattern is determined entirely by one constraint: the observation car at the Pine Valley Junction end of the consist has no rear coupler, and that fact governs every movement the Flyer makes.

The consist is assembled in a fixed order that does not change: observation car — coach — F9A locomotive. The observation car is always at the PVJ end. The F9A is always at the Silver Ridge end. There is no runaround that can change this, and none is needed — the railroad is built around it. The coach handles passenger traffic and express and mail duties from its baggage section. The observation car's open platform faces Pine Valley Junction, which means passengers boarding at PVJ step onto the platform end first — not the worst way to begin a journey.

Departing PVJ southbound on the Scenic Run, the F9A pulls. The consist is drawn out of the station spur — the right-hand spur at Pine Valley Junction, the red depot building at the platform — and eastbound onto the mainline. The F9A leads through the inner tunnel, emerges southwesterly, and pulls the consist down through the National Park District, past the Timberline Fuel Depot turnout, and across the Silver Ridge Gorge bridge. The observation car trails at the rear on this leg, its platform open to the receding view behind the train. The Flyer arrives at Silver Ridge from the west, F9A at the head end, observation car at the platform.

The gorge crossing southbound is experienced from the rear of the train — passengers on the observation platform watch the canyon open up behind them as the consist crosses. It is not the forward view, but it is not a poor substitute. The bridge carries the consist over the river canyon at restricted speed, which is the only appropriate pace for a crossing that most passengers came specifically to see.

Departing Silver Ridge northbound on the Scenic Run, the F9A pushes. The observation car now leads — it exits Silver Ridge first, crosses the Gorge bridge with the open platform facing forward into the canyon, and climbs back through the National Park District toward PVJ. This is the direction in which the observation car earns its name. The forward view through the gorge approach northbound, from an open platform, is the signature experience of the Valley Flyer. The F9A pushes from the Silver Ridge end, invisible to the passengers watching the landscape unfold ahead of them.

The Flyer re-enters the inner tunnel from the south, the observation car leading, and emerges onto the north leg heading toward PVJ. The consist is pushed into the station spur, observation car entering first, F9A last. The round trip is complete with the consist in its correct orientation for the next southbound departure — observation car at the PVJ platform end, F9A ready to pull.

On occasions when the Flyer returns to PVJ via the River Run rather than the Scenic Run — the long way round, up the east leg and through the outer tunnel — the movement begins the same way, F9A pushing northbound out of Silver Ridge. The F9A pulls once the consist is on the east leg heading north, drawing the train up past Timberline Lumber, through the outer tunnel in the northeast corner, and around the north leg loop toward PVJ. The consist is then shoved into the station spur from the mainline, observation car entering first, restoring the correct departure orientation. The River Run return is the exception rather than the rule, but the consist handles it without difficulty.

The Flyer makes no scheduled intermediate stops. On summer excursion runs, the crew may authorize a discretionary photo hold at the Silver Ridge Gorge — a pause long enough for passengers to take in the canyon from the observation platform or, when the platform is in place, to detrain to the viewpoint. The hold may be taken on the mainline with the consist clear of the Silver Ridge turnout fouling point, or the consist may be reversed into the Timberline Fuel Depot spur — observation car entering first regardless of direction — with the spur confirmed clear of spotted tank cars before committing. The F9A pulls the consist back onto the mainline when the stop is complete and resumes in the original direction of travel. The discretionary stop is a crew decision, not a timetable event.

The Valley Flyer holds first-class authority over the Scenic Run for the full duration of its movement. All freight movements must be clear of the mainline before the Flyer's authority window opens. Until the timetable is formalized, the practical authority provisions of Section 7.6 apply and verbal crew coordination governs.

## 8.3. Local Freight — River Run (Timberline Lumber)
The Timberline Lumber local is the River Run's primary freight assignment and one of the simpler movements on the railroad, at least in principle. The work is straightforward — pull loaded cars from the lumber spur, return empties — but the geometry of the spur and the absence of a runaround at Timberline means the move has to be thought through before the engine leaves Pine Valley Junction, in the same way the Mine Run does.

The River Run is the clockwise route, eastbound out of PVJ on the north leg, through the outer tunnel in the northeast corner, emerging southbound on the east leg. Timberline Lumber sits on the east leg with the spur above the mainline on a climbing grade. The freight engine arrives from the north, having come down the east leg from the tunnel. It arrives at Timberline with the spur ahead and to the right.

The current track at Timberline Lumber has a single spur. Until a second spur is in place, the operation runs as a two-visit cycle, parallel to the mine run logic. On the first visit, the road engine pulls the loaded cars off the spur and takes them back to PVJ. On a separate movement — scheduled when traffic permits — the engine returns with empties and spots them to the spur for the next loading cycle. The lumber crew loads on their own schedule; the railroad's job is to ensure empty cars are on the spur when loading is ready and to pull the loads when they are.

The pull is the simpler of the two moves. The road engine arrives light or with an empty consist from PVJ, couples onto the loaded cars on the spur, and departs southbound to continue down the east leg to Silver Ridge, or returns northbound to PVJ depending on the day's work order. Car forwarding from Timberline is typically a PRR flatcar with lumber load outbound and an MTL gondola inbound for lumber waste or secondary material. The waybill governs.

The spotting move requires more care. The road engine arrives from PVJ with empty cars, pulls past the Timberline turnout, reverses, and shoves the empties into the spur. Without a runaround the engine has to push the cars in from whichever direction it arrived — which means the shove is northbound if the engine came down the east leg from the tunnel, or southbound if it came up from Silver Ridge. Either way, the engine ends up at the uphill end of the spur after spotting, which is the correct position for the next pull.

The Timberline Lumber local does not have the drama of the Mine Run or the priority of the Valley Flyer. It is a workday job on a quiet piece of railroad, and it runs on the lumber operation's schedule as much as the railroad's own. When the mill is running, the cars need to be there. That is the whole of it.

## 8.4. Local Freight — Scenic Run (Timberline Fuel Depot)
The Scenic Run local is the railroad's other regular freight assignment, and in some ways the more varied of the two. Where the River Run exists primarily to serve Timberline Lumber, the Scenic Run local works the engine in whichever direction it is traveling, with spots and pulls handled as the work order dictates rather than on a fixed sequence.

The Scenic Run departs PVJ westbound from the yard, enters the inner tunnel on the north leg, and emerges southwesterly to begin the descent toward Silver Ridge. The first customer southbound is the Timberline Fuel Depot. The Fuel Depot receives Shell tank cars primarily, with Dow and Hooker cars as secondary traffic depending on the season and the depot's needs. The spur stub accommodates two tank cars. The engine spots or pulls as the work order requires, clears the turnout, and continues south toward Silver Ridge or returns north to PVJ depending on what remains on the day's list.

Northbound out of Silver Ridge, the same logic applies. The engine works what needs working and leaves what does not. On days when the depot has cars to pull and empties to spot, the Scenic Run local can be the busiest movement on the railroad. On quiet days it may pass this facility without stopping, the work order empty and the mainline clear all the way back to PVJ.

The Valley Flyer's authority over the Scenic Run is the one constraint that governs every Scenic Run freight movement without exception. The local must be clear of the mainline — not merely moving, but clear — before the Flyer's authority window opens. A freight engine spotted in a spur with cars hanging over the mainline is not clear. The Flyer does not slow for freight, and the Scenic Run has no place to hide a consist if the timing is wrong. Until the timetable is formalized, verbal coordination between crews applies.

The Scenic Run local is also the movement most likely to encounter the discretionary Gorge photo stop if the Valley Flyer is running that day. The freight crew should know the Flyer's schedule and plan accordingly — the Gorge and the Fuel Depot spur are both on this line, and a freight engine in the wrong place at the wrong time is an embarrassment the SRRX's flagship passenger service does not deserve.

## 8.5. PVJ Yard Switching
Pine Valley Junction is not a large yard. It was never meant to be. The Silver Ridge Railway exists to serve a mine, a handful of industries, and a passenger run through the mountains, and the yard at PVJ reflects that mission without apology. There are no long classification leads, no dedicated departure tracks, no hump. What there is, is enough — enough track to assemble the day's work, enough room to sort the interchange cuts, and a mainline long enough to serve as the yard's extended arm when the situation calls for it.

No. 98 is the yard engine. The 70-Ton GE handles all switching at PVJ between road assignments, spotting cars to departure tracks, pulling inbound cuts apart, and keeping the station spur clear for the Valley Flyer. It is a capable machine for the work the yard demands of it, and the yard demands nothing it cannot handle. When No. 98 is away on the mine switcher run, the road engine covers any switching that cannot wait, though that situation is uncommon enough that it rarely creates a conflict.

The yard's daily rhythm follows the railroad's traffic. Morning typically begins with No. 98 sorting whatever arrived on the previous day's interchange or inbound freight movements — pulling cars to their departure tracks, spotting empties for the outbound runs, and confirming the station spur is clear and the Valley Flyer consist is properly assembled before the first departure window opens. The Flyer's priority means that nothing else in the yard takes precedence over having the station spur ready on time. A freight car left fouling the station track is No. 98's problem to solve before it becomes the Flyer's problem.

Classification at PVJ is simple by design. The railroad's industries each receive a small number of cars per cycle — rarely more than two or three — and the car forwarding is straightforward enough that a single switcher can sort an entire day's work without difficulty. Cars for the River Run are assembled on one track, cars for the Scenic Run on another, and interchange traffic is held near the NW corner where the D&RGW connection sits. The mainline serves as the sorting lead when the available yard tracks are occupied or when a move requires more room than the classification tracks provide. On a quiet railroad with light traffic, using the mainline for switching is not an embarrassment — it is simply practical.

The interchange at the far northwest corner of the layout is the yard's connection to the outside world. Cars forwarded to the D&RGW are spotted at the interchange track for pickup; inbound cars from the interchange are pulled by No. 98 and sorted into the yard for distribution to the railroad's customers. The interchange is addressed in detail in Section 8.6, but its presence shapes the yard's switching logic — interchange cuts arriving from outside tend to be the largest movements PVJ handles, and on occasions when a cut exceeds the yard's available track length, cars may be set out on the mainline temporarily while No. 98 works the consist down to manageable pieces. Traffic on the north leg is light enough that a brief mainline set-out is an inconvenience rather than a crisis, provided the road crew communicates clearly and clears the mainline before any scheduled movement has authority.

The helper engine pocket on the north side of the yard is No. 98's staging point between the mine helper assignment and yard duty. When No. 98 returns from the mountain it goes to the pocket, not back into the classification tracks. Keeping the helper staged and ready is a discipline that pays off when the road engine is ready to depart for the mine and No. 98 needs to couple on without delay. A switcher that has been pressed into classification work and left on the wrong track is a switcher that makes the Mine Run late.

The warehouse at PVJ sits within the yard and generates its own modest switching requirement — cars spotted for goods transfer to or from the interchange, local freight consignments, and express material that moves through the depot rather than the Flyer's baggage section. The warehouse is not a high-volume customer, but it anchors the yard's identity as something more than a place where trains begin and end. PVJ is a working facility, and the warehouse is part of that work.

The yard is, in the end, a reflection of the railroad it serves. It is not impressive by any measure that a large railroad would apply. But it handles everything the Silver Ridge Railway asks of it, and on a shortline that prides itself on running what it has with what it has, that is the only measure that matters.

## 8.6. D&RGW Interchange
The Silver Ridge Railway does not exist in isolation. Its freight customers ship product to destinations beyond the mountain, and they receive materials from suppliers who have never heard of Pine Valley Junction. The connection that makes this possible is the interchange with the Denver and Rio Grande Western at the far northwest corner of the PVJ yard — a short track at the edge of the SRRX's world where one railroad ends and another begins.

The D&RGW local does not come deep into PVJ. It reaches the interchange track, sets out its cars, picks up whatever the SRRX has forwarded, and departs. What happens on the D&RGW's side of that boundary is not the SRRX's concern. What happens on this side is entirely No. 98's responsibility.

The interchange track is worked at the start and end of each operating session. Inbound cars — those arriving from the D&RGW overnight — are placed on the interchange track before the session begins. They represent whatever the outside world has sent to the SRRX's customers: hoppers for the mine, tank cars for the Smelter or Fuel Depot, revenue freight for Silver Ridge. No. 98 pulls the inbound cut from the interchange track early in the session, works it into the yard, and sorts the cars to their departure tracks for distribution on the day's outbound movements. Outbound cars accumulate on the interchange track as the session progresses — loads pulled from the mine, empties returning to their home roads, through freight destined for points beyond the mountain. At the session's end those cars are removed. The D&RGW local has been and gone.

The D&RGW's physical presence at PVJ is represented by MoPac #4637. The unit stands in for the interchange power — the engine that brought the inbound cut down from the connection and will take the outbound cars when it departs. It sits at the interchange track during the session, visible at the edge of the yard, a reminder that the SRRX is part of a larger railroad world even if that world never intrudes further than the NW corner. #4637 does not work the SRRX yard. It does not pull cars beyond the interchange track. Its job is to be there, and it does that job without complication.

The car forwarding logic is straightforward. Inbound cars are sorted by destination — mine traffic to the mine run consist and Smelter, and Fuel Depot cars to the Scenic Run local, Silver Ridge freight to the River Run or direct to the town switching assignment. Outbound cars are whatever the day's pulls have produced: loaded ore from the mine forwarded to the smelter and then to the interchange, empties returning to their home roads, any through freight the SRRX is handling on behalf of connecting lines. The waybill governs the detail; the interchange track is simply where the handoff happens.

On occasions when an inbound cut is larger than the interchange track can accommodate, cars may be set out on the PVJ mainline temporarily while No. 98 works the consist down to manageable pieces. This is an uncommon situation on a railroad that receives modest traffic, but it is not unheard of — the D&RGW does not always send a short cut, and the SRRX yard is not infinitely accommodating. The mainline is available for brief set-outs provided all movements are coordinated and the track is clear before any road engine has authority.

The interchange is the least dramatic operation on the Silver Ridge Railway. No engine works hard, no grade is climbed, no timetable authority is required. But it is the operation that connects everything else the railroad does to the world beyond Pine Valley Junction, and without it the mine's ore goes nowhere, the Smelter's product sits on the siding, and the SRRX is just a railroad running in circles through the mountains. The interchange track at the NW corner of PVJ is a short piece of rail with an outsized purpose.

## 8.7. Silver Ridge Town Switching
Silver Ridge is the southern terminal of the Silver Ridge Railway and the railroad's most public-facing destination. It began as a mining town, rougher and more functional than it is today, and has evolved over decades into something more complex — a community that serves the national park, welcomes tourists in summer and winter sport visitors in the cold months, houses the railroad's southern workforce, and depends on the SRRX for a category of supply that the mountain road cannot reliably provide. The depot at Silver Ridge is not just a passenger facility. It is the point where the railroad's freight work and its community obligations meet.

The town's road system connects Silver Ridge to the Timberline Fuel Depot, to the lumber operations, and to the national park entrance — a network that functions well enough in good weather and becomes the railroad's argument in bad weather. Fuel arrives by tank car at the Fuel Depot and moves to Silver Ridge by road. Lumber comes down from Timberline the same way. Bulk goods and dry goods arrive by rail at the Silver Ridge depot and are distributed from there. The post office receives its mail from the Flyer's coach baggage section. The brewery takes the occasional grain or hop car when the road from the valley is impassable or the shipment is too large for a truck. The general store orders by the boxcar in late summer, stocking for the winter. None of this is high-volume freight by any railroad's measure, but taken together it represents a community's supply chain, and the SRRX treats it accordingly.

The switching work at Silver Ridge is modest and unhurried. The road engine working the River Run or the Scenic Run handles whatever cars need to be spotted or pulled as part of that movement — there is no dedicated Silver Ridge switcher, and none is needed. A boxcar for the general store, a grain car for the brewery, an empty returned from the previous week's delivery — these are one and two car moves worked at the pace the town sets, not the railroad's. The depot team track accommodates the freight exchange, and the road engine spots or pulls before continuing on its run or returning to PVJ.

The depot itself is the center of the railroad's presence in Silver Ridge. Passenger arrivals and departures on the Valley Flyer are the most visible part of that presence — the Flyer's arrival is something of an event in a town that does not have many — but the depot's work continues between Flyer movements. Express shipments are sorted, mail is handed off to the post office, park service materials are staged for pickup, and the occasional freight car is worked on the team track. The depot agent at Silver Ridge manages all of it, keeping the railroad's southern end running with the same scrappy competence that characterizes the SRRX at every point on its line.

Silver Ridge is also the Flyer's southern home. The consist spends its downtime here between operating sessions, the observation car at the platform end of the station track, the F9A on the far end ready to pull northbound when the next departure comes. The station track must accommodate the full Flyer consist, and like its counterpart at PVJ, it will likely require extension when full-length passenger cars are in service. The mainline runaround at Silver Ridge is available for any movement that requires it — the road engine working the town freight, a meet between opposing movements, or any operational situation that the station track alone cannot resolve.

The town beyond the depot is the reason the railroad matters. The park entrance draws visitors who arrive on the Flyer and leave on it. The hotel and the restaurant fill on summer weekends when the mountain is at its best. The houses along the river, on the good side facing the mountain and the gorge, belong to people who chose Silver Ridge over somewhere easier to reach. The SRRX did not make Silver Ridge what it is. But it is not clear that Silver Ridge would still be what it is without the SRRX, and the railroad understands that. It runs its trains accordingly.
